\section{Reviewing Convergence in Reals} %put \RR here
We'll start by recalling the definition of convergence
of a real sequence $a_n$ in $\RR$.

\begin{definition}[Convergence in $\RR$]
	We say that a sequence $a_n$ converges in $\RR$ if
	there exists some $a \in \RR$ such that for
	any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \NN$ such
	that
	\[|a_n - a| < \varepsilon \quad \text{for any $n > N.$} \]
\end{definition}

The idea for this definition is quite simple, for large
enough $N$, the distance between the terms of the sequence
$a_n$ and $a$ can get arbitrarily small. Essentially,
we are \emph{analyzing} the \textbf{long-term `settling'
	behaviour of the sequence.}

This definition has some problems. First of all, if such
an $a$ exists, then is it unique? Does it even exist? The
answer to the first problem is given by the following
theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness]
	If a real sequence $a_n$ converges to both $a$ and
	$a'$, then $a = a'$. In other words, the limit of the
	sequence is unique.
\end{theorem}

Due to the above theorem, we can now introduce a notation.
If $a_n$ converges to $a$, then we shall write it as
$\displaystyle \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = a$ or $a_n \to a$
as $n \to \infty$.

If you consider a sequence $a_n = (-1)^n$, then it can
be proven that $a_n$ doesn't converge to any real number.
So, given any sequence, it is not necessary that the
sequence must converge.

The majority of real analysis, deals with convergent
sequences. The sequences which don't converge to anything,
treating them becomes harder. Here's a theorem which
has extensive use throughout analysis.

\begin{theorem}[Limit Inequality]\label{thm:lim_ineq}
	Let $a_n$ and $b_n$ be two real sequences such that
	$a_n \ge b_n$ and both the sequences are
	convergent in $\RR$. Then
	\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \ge \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n \]
\end{theorem}

The theorem has a \emph{strong hypothesis;} it assumes
the convergence of both the sequences. If the sequences
are not convergent, then what shall we do? We have
now finally hit the grave the problem, if the sequence ceases
to converge, then is there any useful notion to find where
the sequence is \emph{settling} in the long run?

\subsection{A Grave Problem}
\begin{example}[A Really (Un?)Settling Sequence]\label{example:multiple_accumulation_points}
	Consider the sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ given by
	\[a_n = (-1)^{n}\left( 2 + \frac 1 n \right).  \]
	Is $a_n$ convergent? Does it \emph{seem to converge}
	anywhere?
\end{example}

For the beginners, it is a good exercise to sit down and
write a formal proof that the sequence doesn't converge.
Although, if you take large $n$, then one realizes
that $a_n$ seems to sometimes approach $2$ but sometimes
approaches $-2$. Here's a small table, for values of $n$
around $1000$.
\[
	\begin{array}{c|c}
		n    & a_n             \\ \hline
		997  & -2.001003\ldots \\
		998  & 2.001002\ldots  \\
		999  & -2.001001\ldots \\
		1000 & 2.001000\ldots  \\
		1001 & -2.000999\ldots \\
		1002 & 2.000998\ldots  \\
		1003 & -2.000997\ldots
	\end{array}
\]

Formally, we can write it as
\[\lim_{n \to \infty} a_{2n} = 2 \quad \text{\&} \quad \lim_{n \to \infty} a_{2n-1} = -2.\]

This means that even though the sequence doesn't
converge(due to \Cref{thm:subseq_conv}), there is still some
settling behaviour in the sequence. To analyze the
long term `settling' behaviour, we definitely want to
capture this behaviour.

\subsection{Important Terminologies}
With the motivation set in space, we'll now introduce a new
definition.

\begin{definition}[Accumulation Points]
	For a real valued sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$, we
	say that $u$ is an \emph{accumulation point} if
	for any $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists infinitely
	many $m \in \NN$
	such that
	\[a_m \in (u-\varepsilon, u + \varepsilon). \]
\end{definition}

In essence, this definition says that there are infinitely
members of sequence $a_n$ which are lying arbitrarily
arbitrarily close to $u$. A similar definition which
textbooks use is the following.

\begin{definition}[Subsequential Limits]
	We say $u$ is a subsequential limit of sequence
	$\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ if there exists some subsequence
	$\{a_{n_k}\}_{k \ge 1}$ such that $a_{n_k} \to u$ as
	$n \to \infty$.
\end{definition}

Its not a surprise that any accumulation point must be a
subsequential limit of the sequence. Think like this,
any subsequential limit MUST be an accumulation
point by very definition of convergence. If you have
accumulation point in hand, then first pick an element
within $2^{-1}$ neighborhood of $u$. Then pick a new
element from $2^{-2}$ neighborhood. Rinse and repeat!
This way one can construct a subsequence converging to
$u$. Details are obviously missing. Thus, we shall
formally state it and prove it as a theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:accum-pt_is_subseq_lim}
	$v \in \RR$ is an \emph{accumulation point} if and
	only if $v$ is a \emph{subsequential limit}.
\end{theorem}

With help of above theorem, we can now use the words
\emph{accumulation points} and \emph{subsequential limits}

trivial.

\begin{theorem}
	A sequence $\{a_n\}$ is converges to $a\in \RR$ if and
	only if $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ has only a unique
	\emph{accumulation point}, $a$ to be particular.
\end{theorem}

The idea of $\limsup$ is exactly the same as finding
the ``largest'' accumulation point. Its a good time to
revisit \Cref{example:multiple_accumulation_points}.

We could clearly clearly see that $a_n$ was kind of
settling around the points $2$ and $-2$. Indeed, due to
\Cref{thm:accum-pt_is_subseq_lim} both $2$ and $-2$ are
\emph{accumulation points}. It requires some work to show
that there are no other accumulation points other than
than $2,-2$.
\begin{exercise}
	Show that the sequence
	\[a_n = (-1)^{n}\left( 2 + \frac 1 n \right)\]
	has only two \emph{accumulation points}, which are
	$2$ and $-2$.
\end{exercise}

The largest \emph{accumulation point} of $a_n$ is $2$, thus
we would write $\limsup a_n = 2$. There are some technical
details which one needs to address, like its existence
and whether a true maximum\footnote{if you consider a
	set of all accumulation points, it could be the
	case a \emph{supremum} exists, but a maximum doesn't.}
exists or not. The next section is dedicated to properly
establishing this notion and filling any holes of this
concept which we have in mind.

\section{Introducing Limit Superior}

We'll walk through a few equivalent definitions and give
the proper proofs wherever necessary. We will start with
the subsequential limits definition and then move onto
the more \emph{harder} definition which allows for easier
computation of the limit superior.

\begin{center}
	{\color{red}\textbf{Note:}} All the sequences
	are \textbf{bounded} unless stated otherwise.
\end{center}

\subsection{Largest Accumulation Point/Subsequential Limit}
We have now seen that the limit of the sequence is indeed
an \emph{accumulation point}. The idea of limit supremum
is finding the largest \emph{accumulation point} of
your $a_n$. There is no guarantee whether a true
`maximum' exists or not. Therefore, for sequence
$a_{n}$ define

\[V \coloneqq \{x \in \RR : x\text{ is an \emph{accumulation point} of } a_n\}. \]

We want to find $\sup(V)$, for which we shall use
\nameref{axiom:completeness}. We need to ensure that
$V$ is bounded above and non-empty. The following
lemma will do it.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:subseq_lim_bounded-non-empty}
	$V$ is bounded above and non-empty.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let $u \in \RR$ be an \emph{accumulation point} or a
	\emph{subsequential limit}. Thus, there exists some
	subsequence $\{a_{n_k}\}_{k \ge 1}$ such that
	$a_{n_k} \to u$ as $k \to \infty$. Since $a_n$ is
	bounded, there exists some $M > 0$ such that
	$-M \le a_n \le M$ for all $n \ge 1$. Therefore,
	\[-M \le a_{n_k} \le M \quad \text{for all $k \ge 1$.}\]
	Taking $m \to \infty$ on both the sides with aid of
	\Cref{thm:lim_ineq}, we shall $|u| < M$ for any
	accumulation point $u$ of $a_n$, proving that
	$V$ \textbf{is bounded}.

	Since $a_n$ is bounded, we know that via
	\nameref{thm:bolzano_weirstrass}, there exists a
	subsequence $\{a_{m_k}\}_{k \ge 1}$ such that
	$a_{m_k} \to t$
	for some $t \in \RR$. Since $t$ is a \emph{subsequential
		limit}, it must be an \emph{accumulation point}, which
	shows $t \in V \implies V \ne \emptyset$.
\end{proof}

With this preparatory lemma, we are now free to define
the following.
\begin{definition}
	For a bounded real sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$,
	define
	\[\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = \sup(V)\]
	where $V$ is set of all accumulation points of
	$\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark*}[Notation]
	There are several notations for this quantity. People
	write it as $\varlimsup a_n$ or just $\limsup a_n$.
	We shall justify it later why we use $n \to \infty$
	below $\limsup$ in a latter section.
\end{remark*}

We have finally setup the definition of the limit
superior. A question still remains\dots Is the supremum
attainable? The answer turns out to be a \textbf{yes.}
The following lemma is from Rudin but is very crucial
to get a better understanding about the `distribution' of
\emph{accumulation points} on the real line.

If a student wants to freshen up on the concept of what a
`closed' set is, then they can refer \textbf{here} in the
appendix.

\subsection{The Epsilon Criteria}

In this subsection, we will carry-forward and build up
more on the previous definition of limit superior.
We wish to analyze what kind of behaviour does the
largest \emph{accumulation point} exhibits.

What happens when you nudge the $\limsup$ slightly?
Let $s^\ast = \limsup a_n$, what happens if you consider
which is slightly greater than $s^\ast$? For now,
$y = s^\ast + \epsilon$ for some $\epsilon > 0$. Since it
is greater than $\sup(V)$, $y$ is definitely \textbf{NOT}
an \emph{accumulation point}.

Recall via the proof of $V$ being non-empty and bounded,
if there are infinitely many terms greater than $y$,
then via \nameref{thm:bolzano_weirstrass}, we will get
an \emph{accumulation point} strictly greater than
$s^\ast$, which is absurd since $s^\ast$ is the maximum
such real. Thus, we may conclude that there are only
finitely $n \in \NN$ such that $y = s^\ast + \varepsilon
	> a_n$.

What if you nudge the $s^\ast$ backwards? This time,
let $z = s^\ast - \varepsilon$. By the very definition
of $\sup(V)$, there exists some $v \in V$ such that
$z < v \le s^\ast$. From here, its easy to conclude that
there are inifinitely many $n$ such that
$z = s^\ast - \varepsilon < a_n$. All our discussion till
now, can be summarized in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}
	For any bounded real sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$, let
	$s^\ast = \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n$, then
	\begin{itemize}
		\item for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there are
		      finitely many $n$ satisfying
		      $s^\ast + \varepsilon < a_n$;
		\item for any $\varepsilon > 0$, there are
		      infinitely many $n$ satisfying
		      $s^\ast - \varepsilon < a_n$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
	To be added\dots
\end{proof}

The above theorem is \textbf{extremely useful} when dealing
with $\limsup$ or $\liminf$(with the appropriate changes).
The above two properties are quite unique, in the sense
only the $\limsup$ satisfies both the above properties
together. Let's state this ``uniqueness'' property clearly.

\begin{lemma}
	For a bounded sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$, if
	$s^\ast \in \RR$ such that
	\begin{itemize}
		\item For any $\varepsilon > 0$, there are
		      finitely many $n$ satisfying
		      $s^\ast + \varepsilon < a_n$;
		\item For any $\varepsilon > 0$, there are
		      infinitely many $n$ satisfying
		      $s^\ast - \varepsilon < a_n$.
	\end{itemize}
	Then there is only one $s^\ast \in \RR$ which
	satisfies both of these properties.
\end{lemma}

\end{proof}

Magic! We already know that $\limsup a_n$ satisfies both
of the properties and due to the above lemma, we now know
$\limsup a_n$ is the only number which can satisfy both the
properties together. Therefore, the concept of
$\limsup$ could be purely defined in terms of this
$\varepsilon$-crteria. We won't do that here, but the
theorem stated above will be a very important result which
will be consitently used in proofs regarding $\limsup$
and $\liminf$ later on.

\subsection{Useful Results}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:limsup_liminf_equal}
	For any real valued bounded sequence
	$\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$,
	\[\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n = \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n = a \iff \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = a. \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Monotonicity]
	Let $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ and $\{b_n\}_{n \ge 1}$
	satisfy $a_n \ge b_n$ for all $n \ge 1$. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n & \ge \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n \\
		\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n & \ge \liminf_{n \to \infty} b_n
	\end{align*}
\end{theorem}


\begin{theorem}\label{thm:bounds_on_limsup}
	Let $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ be a real-valued bounded
	sequence and $x \in \RR$ be some real number, then the
	following statements are true.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n > x$ iff there
		      exists some $\varepsilon > 0$ such that
		      $a_n > x + \varepsilon$ for infinitely many
		      $n \in \NN$;
		\item $\liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n < x$ iff there
		      exists some $\varepsilon > 0$ such that
		      $a_n < x - \varepsilon$ for infinitely many
		      $n \in \NN$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Sub-additivity]
	For two real bounded sequences $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$
	and $\{b_n\}_{n \ge 1}$, we must have
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $\displaystyle \limsup_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n + \limsup_{n \to \infty} b_n$
		\item $\displaystyle \liminf_{n \to \infty} (a_n + b_n) \ge \liminf_{n \to \infty} a_n + \liminf_{n \to \infty} b_n$
	\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Heuristics: When to use it?}

There are quite a few applications.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item One can solve problems related related to
	      finding the limit superior and limit inferior.
	      This sounds artificial but it is definitely
	      an application.

	\item $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ always exist! So,
	      whenever we are solving a problem, we
	      can't apply theorems like \Cref{thm:lim_ineq}.
	      Although, any theorem regarding $\limsup$
	      and $\liminf$ can be applied almost everywhere!

	\item This is a very useful tool for showing that
	      the limit exists, courtesy to
	      \Cref{thm:limsup_liminf_equal}. This tool
	      which we have developed can be replaced by
	      first principles(some $\varepsilon-\delta$
	      arguments) rather than analyzing the
	      \emph{accumulation points}. Although, thinking
	      in terms of \emph{accumulation points} hugely
	      simplifies our proofs and is a good direction
	      to think in.

	\item The next section will present some theorems
	      where this new notion of $\limsup$ makes
	      stating these results extremely easy. The root
	      and ratio tests for convergence of series is a
	      very good example which we shall cover later.
\end{enumerate}

To put it in a few words, \emph{if you don't know whether
	the limit exists or not, then take the help of your
	best ally,} the limit superior.

\section{Brief Note on Extended Reals and Unbounded Sequences}
Throughout the article, we have only dealt with
bounded sequences. What happens if the sequence is
unbounded? The `largest' \emph{accumulation point} of a
sequence breaks down\dots If you try to imitate the proof
\Cref{lem:subseq_lim_bounded-non-empty}, showing $V$
bounded is not possible since a subsequence might
converge to $\pm\infty$.

Therefore, if the sequence is unbounded we would like to
work with extended reals, which is
\[\ol\RR \coloneqq \RR \cup \{ +\infty, -\infty \} \]

When working with extended reals, we know that
$\sup(V)$ always exists(just need to ensure
$V \ne \emptyset$) in $\ol\RR$. There are quite a few
conventions and definitions one needs to know when
working with extended reals, but the following will
suffice.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item $0\times \pm\infty$ and $\pm\infty \times 0$
	      are undefined.

	\item $\infty - \infty$ is undefined.

	\item $\infty + a = a + \infty = \infty$ for all
	      $a \in \RR$.

	\item $a + (-\infty) = (-\infty) + a = -\infty$ for
	      $a \in \RR$.
\end{enumerate}

The discussion for unbounded sequences is rather trivial,
we've dealt with the non-trivial part already. Therefore,

\subsection{An Example from Probability Theory}
This one is too niche, regardless a good example.
If one is not aware of the notion of
weak convergence/convergence in distribution in
probability theory, then feel free to skip this one
for now.

\begin{prob*}{(No Atoms in Weak Convergence)}{}
	Let $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \mathbb P)$ be a
	probability space and $\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ be a sequence
	of real valued-random variables and $X$ be another
	random variable such that $X_n \overset{d}{\to} X$.
	If $a$ is a continuity point of $F_X$, then prove that
	\[\lim_{m \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(X_m = a) = \mathbb{P}(X = a) = 0.  \]
\end{prob*}

\begin{sol}
	Let $F_n$ denote the CDF of $X_n$. Since $F_n$ is
	monotonically increasing, there are only countable
	discontinuities of $F_n$. Therefore, we may choose
	a strictly decreasing sequence
	$\{\delta_m\}_{m \ge 1}$ approaching $0$ such that
	$a-\delta_m$ and $a+\delta_m$ are both continuity
	points $F_X$ for all $m \ge 1$. Observe,
	\begin{align*}
		\PP(X_n = a) & \le \PP\left(X_n \in (a-\delta_m, a+\delta_m]\right) \\
		             & = F_n(a+\delta_m) - F_n(a - \delta_m)
	\end{align*}
	Since $a+\delta_m$ and $a-\delta_m$
	were chosen to be continuity points of $F_X$, the
	limit as $n \to \infty$ of $F_n(a+\delta_m)$
	and $F_n(a - \delta_m)$ exists and is precisely
	$F_X(a + \delta_m)$ and $F_X(a - \delta_m)$.
	Taking $\limsup_{n \to \infty}$ on both the sides
	with help of \Cref{thm:limsup_liminf_equal}, we get
	\[\limsup_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) \le F_X(a + \delta_m) - F_X(a - \delta_m) \]
	for all $m \ge 1$. Once again, since $a+\delta_m$
	and $a - \delta_m$ are continuity points of $F_X$,
	if we take $m \to \infty$ on both the sides,
	$F_X(a + \delta_m) \to F_X(a)$ and
	$F_X(a - \delta_m) \to F_X(a)$ since $a$ is a point
	of continuity. This yields
	\[\limsup_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) \le 0 \implies \limsup_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) = 0 \]
	Since $\PP(\bullet) \ge 0$, taking $\liminf$ both
	sides gives
	\[0 \le \liminf_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) \le \limsup_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) = 0,  \]
	forcing both $\limsup$ and $\liminf$ to be
	equal to $0$, which proves
	\[\lim_{n \to \infty} \PP(X_n = a) = 0 \]
	completing the proof.
\end{sol}

\subsection{Analysis Problems}
\begin{problem}[Alternate Definition]
Let $\{x_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ be a bounded real sequence.
We now define
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The limit superior of $x_n$ is the
	      infimum of set $V$ of $v \in \RR$ such that
	      $v < x_n$ for at most a finite number of
	      $n \in \NN$.

	\item The limit inferior of $x_n$ is the supremum of
	      the set $W$ of $w$ such that $x_m < w$ for at
	      most a finite number of $m \in \NN$.
\end{enumerate}
Show that the above definition is the same as tail-end
definition of $\limsup$ which we have discussed earlier.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[Equality Case]
Let $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ be a convergent sequence with $a_n \to a \in \mathbb{R}$, and let $(b_n)$ be any real sequence. Prove that
\[
	\limsup_{n\to\infty} (a_n + b_n) = a + \limsup_{n\to\infty} b_n
\]
and
\[
	\liminf_{n\to\infty} (a_n + b_n) = a + \liminf_{n\to\infty} b_n.
\]
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Let $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ and $\{b_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ be sequences of real numbers. Suppose $a_n \to a$ with $a > 0$. Prove that
\[
	\limsup_{n\to\infty} (a_n b_n) = a \limsup_{n\to\infty} b_n
\]
and
\[
	\liminf_{n\to\infty} (a_n b_n) = a \liminf_{n\to\infty} b_n.
\]
What happens if $a < 0$? What if $a = 0$?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
This problem is closely related to \Cref{thm:bounds_on_limsup}. For bounded sequence $\{a_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ and
$a \in \RR$, show that

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n \ge a \iff$ for all
	      $\varepsilon > 0$ we have
	      $a_n > a - \varepsilon$ for infinitely many
	      $n \in \NN$;

	\item $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n \le a \iff$ for
	      all $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists some
	      $N \in \NN$ such that we have
	      $a_n \le a + \varepsilon$ for $n > N$;

	\item $\limsup_{n \to \infty} a_n < a \iff$ there
	      exists $\varepsilon > 0$ and $N \in \NN$ such 
		  that $a_n \le a - \varepsilon$ for $n > N$.
\end{itemize}
\end{problem}

\subsection{Probability Problems}
\begin{problem}[First Borel-Cantelli Lemma]
Given a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$
and a sequence events $\{A_n\}_{n \ge 1}$ where
$A_m \in \mathcal F$ for all $m \in \NN$, then prove
that

\[\sum_{i = 1}^\infty \PP(A_i) < \infty \implies \PP\left(\limsup_{n \to \infty} A_n\right) = 0.\]
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
For a sequence of random variables $\{X_n\}_{n \ge 1}$
in the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal F, \PP)$
satisfying
\[\PP(X_n < a \,\,\text{i.o. in $n$ and } X_n > b\,\,\text{i.o. in $n$}) = 0,\]
prove that $X_n$ converges almost surely.
\end{problem}

\subsection{Standard Facts from Analysis-I}

\begin{definition}[Limit Points]
	$u \in \RR$ is said to be a \emph{limit point} of
	$A \subseteq \RR$ if for any $\varepsilon > 0$,
	there exists some $u^\ast \in A$ such that
	\[u^\ast \in (u - \varepsilon, u + \varepsilon)\setminus \{u\}. \]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Closed Sets]
	A subset $A \subseteq \RR$ is said to
	\emph{closed set} if it contains all its limit points.
\end{definition}

\begin{axiom}[The Completeness Axiom]\label{axiom:completeness}
	For any non-empty $A \subset \RR$ bounded above, the
	supremum of $A$ or $\sup(A)$ exists in $\RR$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{theorem}[Subsequences of Convergence Sequences]\label{thm:subseq_conv}
	If $a_n \to a$ as $n \to \infty$, then any
	subsequence of $a_n$ must converge to $a$ as well.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Bolzano Weirstrass]\label{thm:bolzano_weirstrass}
	For any bounded sequence $\{b_n\}_{n \ge 1}$, there
	exists a convergent subsequence
	$\{b_{n_k}\}_{k \ge 1}$.
\end{theorem}
