# Basic Definitions
I shall assume that the reader is faimiliar with the notion
of orders and knows a proof to Fermat's Little Theorem.
Recall the definition of primitive root modulo $p$.

<Definition title="Primitive Roots">

We say that $a$ is a primitive root $\bmod \,p$ if
$\ord_p(a) = p-1$.
</Definition>

Also, recall the following useful lemma.

<Lemma>

If $a^n \equiv 1 \pmod p$ then $\ord_p(a) \mid n$.
In particular, due to Fermat's Little Theorem we have
$\ord_p(a) \mid p-1$.
</Lemma>

Therefore, $a$ is a primitive root if it hits its maximal
possible order. There are even more ways to think about
it, for example, if $a$ is a primitive root, then
$\{a,a^2,\dots,a^{p-1} = 1 \} = \{ 1, 2, 3, \dots, p-1 \}$
where the elements are taken modulo $p$. Thus, one may 
say that powers of $a$ generate the entire residue set 
modulo $p$.

## A Rigorous Setup

In high-school, we consider $1,2,\dots, p-1$ as remainders
modulo $p$, but as one shifts to higher-math, we would
like to treat it as more _concrete_ mathematical objects. 
This is basically achieved by considering the quotient
group $(\ZZ/p\ZZ)^\ast$ which consists of elements
$\{\ol{1}, \ol 2, \dots, \ol{p-1} \}$, basically the 
non-zero cosets of $p\ZZ$. We wish to show that this 
group is cyclic under the operation of $\times$(we might
use the notation of $\cdot$).

The cooler fact is, that while working $\bmod\, p$ inverses
always exist. So, as a matter of fact the tuple 
$(\ZZ/p\ZZ, +, \times)$ forms a field. We denote this 
field by $\FF_p$, that is a finite field with $p$ elements. 

# Preliminaries

We'd need a few results from groups and fields before
going into the proof of the main result. The first result 
is called the Lagrange's Theorem in context of polynomials
of fields. Don't confuse it between the theorem of 
number of cosets and the index of a subgroup. The latter 
theorem is a standard lemma in books like Dummit and Foote.

<Theorem title="Lagrange's Theorem">

For any non-constant polynomial $f \in \FF_p[x]$, there are 
at most $\deg(f)$ solutions to the equation $f(x) = 0$ for 
$x \in \FF_p$.

</Theorem>

<div class="proof">

We shall prove the result by induction on $\deg(n)$. For
the case, we have the lienar equation $ax + b = 0$ for 
$a,b \in \FF_p$ where $a \ne 0$. By existence of unique
multiplicative inverses in $\FF_p$, we know that

$$
ax + b = 0 \iff x = -b\cdot a^{-1}
$$

which resolves the base case. As an induction hypothesis,
assume that any polynomial $Q \in \FF_p[x]$ with
$\deg(Q) = n$ has at most $n$ solutions. We now need to 
show that for any polynomial $P \in \FF_p[x]$ with 
$\deg(P) = n + 1$, it has at most $n+1$ solutions.

If $P$ has no roots, then we are done. If $P$ has at least
one root, call it $\gamma \in \FF_p$, then by Factor Theorem
over any ring, we may write
$$
P(x) = (x-a)\cdot T(x)
$$
for $T \in \FF_p[x]$ where $\deg(T) = n$. Applying the 
induction hypothesis on $T$, we know that $T$ has at most
$n$ roots. Adding one more root $a$, we know that $P$ can 
have at most $n+1$ roots, which completes the induction 
and the proof to the theorem. $\blacksquare$

</div>


<Lemma title="Order of Higher Powers">

For a group $G$ and $x \in G$, if $\ord(x) = n < \infty$ 
for some $x \in G$, then
$$
\ord(x^a) = \dfrac{n}{\gcd(n,a)}
$$
for any $a \in \ZZ \setminus \{ 0\}$.

</Lemma>

I will leave this lemma unproven. It is a good exercise 
for the readers to show the above result.

# The Main Proof

Let's state the theorem formally before we get into the
proof.

<Theorem title="Generator Modulo p">

$(\ZZ/p\ZZ)^\ast$ is cyclic over the operation $\times$.
</Theorem>

<div class="proof">
By Fermat's Little Theorem, we know that 

$$
\FF_p[x] \ni g(x) = x^{p-1} - 1
$$

has exactly $p-1$ roots in $\FF_p$.

<Claim> $x^d - 1$ has exactly $d$ roots in $\FF_p$ where $d \mid p-1$. </Claim>

<div class="proof">
The proof to this claim is simple, just observe
$$
x^{p-1} - 1 = x^{dk} - 1 = (x^d-1)(x^{d(k-1)}+x^{d(k-2)} + \dots + 1)
$$
where we write $p = dk - 1$ for some $k \in \ZZ$. By
Lagrange's Theorem, $x^d - 1$ must have exactly $d$
solutions. $\square$
</div>

Define $A_d = \{x \in \FF_p: x \ne 0, \ord(x) = d \}$ for
$d \mid n$. For any $a \in A_d$, consider a subgroup 
$H = \{ a, a^2, \dots, a^d = 1 \}$. Since, any $y \in H$
satisfies $y^d - 1 = 0$, we can deduce that any element 
of order $d$ must be a power of $a$. Since element
$t \in (\ZZ/p\ZZ)^\ast$ can have order $d$ only if it is a 
power of $a$, then $t$ must be in $H$. Therefore, we now
need to find the number of elements in $A_d$ which have
order exactly $d$. For any $t \in H$(write $t = a^k$), 
$$
\ord(t) = \ord(a^k) = \dfrac{d}{\gcd(d,k)} = d \iff \gcd(d,k) = 1
$$

which gives $|A_d| = \varphi(d)$ where $\varphi$ is the 
euler's function, which counts number of elements less than
$d$ and relatively prime to $d$.

Simply taking $d = p - 1$ to get $|A_{p-1}| = \varphi(p-1) \geq 1$ would be wrong since we assumed $A_d \neq \emptyset$ by taking $a \in A_d$. Assume $|A_{d'}| = 0$ for some $d' \mid n$, then
$$
    p - 1 = \sum_{d \mid p-1} |A_d| = \sum_{\substack{d \mid p-1 \\ d \neq d'}} |A_d| = \sum_{\substack{d \mid p-1 \\ d \neq d'}} \varphi(d) < \sum_{d \mid p-1} \varphi(d) = p - 1
$$
where we have used the fact that $\sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n$ at the end.

The first equality true because every element has some
order and must lie in exactly $A_d$. The second equality is 
just removing that that $0$ element since $|A_{d'}| = 0$. 
The contradiction is obvious, which finishes the proof. $\blacksquare$
</div>

## Remarks
We not only proved that there exists at least one primitive 
root modulo $p$, but we also proved that there are exactly 
$\varphi(\varphi(p)) = \varphi(p-1)$ primitive roots
modulo $p$. 

Also, Lagrange's Theorem isn't anything special for the 
field $\FF_p$. Replacing by any $\FF$ also works. We just
highlighted the fact because it could fail over certain 
rings. For example, consider the polynomial $f(x) = x^2-1$
over the ring $(\ZZ/8\ZZ)^\ast$ over the operation $\times$.
It has 4 roots, namely $1,3,5,7$.
